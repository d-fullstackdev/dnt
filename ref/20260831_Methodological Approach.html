Green Investment List

The ultimate goal is to create an independent, evidence-based green investment list to show how Danantara Indonesia could use its role to mobilize co-investment, de-risk early-stage infrastructure, improve SOE governance, and avoid locking in emissions-intensive assets.

The value, however, is not only the project list but also the reasoning behind why each opportunity qualifies, what risks must be managed, and which projects should be excluded even if they are politically attractive.

This study begins with sectors such as energy, agriculture, downstream industries, housing, infrastructure, and manufacturing because these sectors mirror how a national development agenda typically organizes priorities and project pipelines. There is also strong evidence that these sectors are central to Indonesian growth, jobs, food security, urbanization, exports, and industrial policy.

Agriculture, combined with forestry and fisheries, contributed about 13.1% of Indonesian GDP. Its significance is deeply rooted in rural livelihoods, food supply, and employment. A strong agricultural sector not only supports food security but also provides a foundation for a strong manufacturing sector.

Manufacturing is pivotal for technology advancement, value addition, employment, exports, import substitution, and the government’s downstreaming agenda.

Construction and infrastructure enable everything from housing provision and logistics to public services, urban growth, and private investment. Meanwhile, all of these activities depend on energy.

Danantara itself publicly articulated that its focus spans renewables and energy transition, food security, infrastructure, minerals and downstreaming, industrial estates, and manufacturing-related value chains (Li et al. 2026).

In an effort to create a list of green investments for Danantara Indonesia, this study uses a decision system that tests whether an investment is genuinely low-carbon, financially resilient, socially fair, and independently verifiable.

This study starts by creating an inventory of green projects already announced by the Indonesian government and/or state-owned enterprises from 2020 onwards. A practical workflow for building such a list appears below.

  • 1. Map Indonesia’s investable green and transition opportunities by creating an inventory of green projects announced by state-owned enterprises from 2020 onward and all projects confirmed by Danantara Indonesia since its launch in 2025.

  • 2. Sort each activity into six sectors, namely energy, agriculture, downstream industries, housing, infrastructure, and manufacturing. This study will also sort to exclude activities involving fossil lock-in, deforestation, and social harm.

  • 3. Screen projects against agreed principles and measures, such as the Indonesia Taxonomy for Sustainable Finance (Version 3) and ASEAN Taxonomy for Sustainable Finance (Version 4), while considering Indonesia’s commitments, such as the 2030 NDC emissions target, net zero, national emissions peak, the FOLU Net Sink 2030, its own National Energy Policy, and JETP.

  • 4. Compare the projects with Danantara’s existing portfolio and merge the ones that meet the green criteria.

  • 5. Build the green investment list.

This study does not consider or recommend projects such as geothermal power plants, nuclear power plants, and coal repurposing as “green”; unless the projects are meant to retire existing plants. Otherwise, projects that fall under categories such as “coal repurposing” or the oxymoron “green coal” will be immediately excluded from the list.

This study also argues that renewable status alone does not make a project fair or sustainable. We focus on pathways to net-zero that do not harm Indigenous livelihoods, biodiversity, or affected communities.

A delicate approach is required when pursuing solutions such as “biofuel” to ensure production without clearing natural forest, converting peatlands, displacing food production, violating land rights or labor standards, or having weak traceability.

Building Green Investment List

The main principle used in this report is additionality, as Norfund1 also upholds. The Norwegian fund ensures its capital contributes to outcomes that would not have happened otherwise.

Danantara should adopt an additionality principle because it would ensure that state capital produces public and economic outcomes that ordinary markets would not deliver on their own. Without this principle, Danantara risks becoming merely a large allocator of public assets into already-bankable projects instead of a catalyst for structural transformation.
